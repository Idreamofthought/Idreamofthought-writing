Thoughts on Talent

22 November 2026 — Richard Jenkins · Philosophy & Ideas · Essay

Perhaps “talent” isn’t the right word, but I had to start somewhere. I’ve often observed that talented people shine brightly. Perhaps everyone shines in their own way. But if our light becomes a glare, our talents lose focus. They can drain us or burn others.

When we shine, we also cast shadows. Unless we accept those shadows and explore them instead of fearing them, we may find they absorb our light.

Many gifted people live with struggles the public sees only in part. Some find a way through with help; others die young. Their lives cannot be reduced to their struggles, and from outside we rarely know the full shape of their shadows.

Talent isn’t perfect or constant. Writers stare at blank pages; singers struggle to find the music. Even the humdrum world of everyday life finds shadows tugging at its sleeves.

Then there are the things others do that hurt us, and the things we do that hurt others. We can become jealous of one another.

We put talent on a pedestal and gaze up at it. Then we shake and rock the pedestal until the person standing on it is damaged or destroyed. Sometimes we only recognise the importance of an artist’s light after their death, in the music they leave behind.

Sometimes people who cannot face their own shadows try to expose yours, spreading rumours, lies and gossip. When we judge others, especially those who can no longer answer us, we should distinguish what is documented from what we assume. But caution must not become silence: harm must be named, and those who were hurt must be heard.

We also need to be honest and unflinching when judging ourselves, while remaining forgiving and kind.

Sometimes we sabotage ourselves, blinding ourselves with our own light.

I don’t have the answers for managing the light we shine. We have to let it shine, of course, but we also need to turn off the torch and recharge the batteries. We need focus and direction, so our light doesn’t become a glare. Perhaps we need a healthy outlet, such as sport or writing.

We need to look at the tendrils of shadow around us and accept our imperfections and failures. Yes, my actions that day were not what they should have been. But we can learn from them. What can we do to change the past?

Talent isn’t easy to handle or manage. Why should it be? It’s a moving blob of light inside us all.

Talent can become intoxicating, even toxic—a shadow over us all—when the harmful actions of talented people go unchallenged.

Abbé Pierre and Jimmy Savile spring to mind. Their cases and the evidence around them are different, but both remind us that admired public work cannot erase harm. We can’t weigh good and bad on scales and say the good outweighs the bad. Trauma and damage cannot be ignored. We can’t say, “We can ignore this person’s abuse because they raised millions.” To do so makes us morally questionable at best, and morally blind at worst.

None of us is perfect, but our failures and harms are not all the same. That is the challenge, the dilemma. Can we be rigorous enough to resist? And when we fail to resist, what can we do? How can we help ourselves and others in the candy shop of the world?

The shades of shadow—from a child’s mischief to crimes against humanity—can blur before our eyes. The light gets lost in the swirling fog, or we tell people to look at the light while we do evil things hidden in the shadows.

This is where we lose our humanity: by making excuses for our behaviour, or by neglecting ourselves until illnesses like addiction overtake our light. It’s tragic. I offer no blame for the struggle. But as people and as a society, we must challenge unacceptable behaviour as soon as we become aware of it, or risk dire consequences. We need to question ourselves instead of inventing a version that never happened. We need to find ways to live with both our light and our shadows.

For everyone has fallen from grace.
