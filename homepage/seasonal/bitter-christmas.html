Bitter Christmas

Christmas is a hotchpotch of borrowed ideas and cultures, pinned to a board like an exotic butterfly, then showered with glitter and expectancy like the tackiest chocolate box ever made.

We build up our expectations by decorating our homes to chase away the darkness and add a little hope, so that Tiny Tim does not die in our hearts. We try to keep alive a broken magic that evaporates in the glee of children’s eyes, then breaks against reality when belief gives way and Santa is not real.

We hope for joy through sharing and giving, but family and friends can be like fish: after a while, it starts to stink.

Some families put a huge spread on the table and a huge smear on the bank balance just for this. Every Christmas, we wheel out the bulging bin of waste and wonder if it is really reasonable to carry on like this. Then we head back to our house and carry on with the feast. Christmas seems to start earlier and last longer every year, with Christmas sections in some famous shops opening after the summer holidays—Harrods, for example, opens its Christmas section in August—because it is a guaranteed way to drum up trade.

Struggling families get into debt; worse, middle-class families risk spoiling everyone, and rich folk just do it anyway.

It is a smorgasbord orgy of whimsy and waste, peppered with drunken arguments, long-forgotten vibes to pick up, and axes to grind, all dragged into a day of toil and cooking.

We watch as our family puts its feet under the table and then get annoyed when they treat us like servants. A week later, we do it all again for New Year.
